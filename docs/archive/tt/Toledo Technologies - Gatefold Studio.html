A1Liner Notes

A studio for the
slow, careful kind
of software.

We build, fix, and ship the code that runs underneath the rest of your business. No agency theatre. No quarterly roadmaps written in passive voice.

Most technical work today moves at the speed of a tweet. A bug ticket gets routed through five tools and three time zones. A feature request comes back as a Notion doc. Months later, somebody finally ships something nobody wants to maintain. We started Toledo Technologies because we wanted to do the opposite of all of that.

The studio is small on purpose. Senior people only. The same person who scopes your work writes the code, opens the pull request, and stays on the phone when the deploy goes sideways at [11:47 PM]. There is no junior tier here, because the junior tier is where most projects go to die.

What you'll find on this record

Side A — the side you're reading — is the studio itself. Who we are, how we work, and the four kinds of jobs we take. Side B is the catalog: deploy-ready repositories you can buy outright, plus a small shelf of case studies for the systems we've pulled out of the fire.

The whole thing is meant to be read like a printed liner. There are no sidebars trying to capture your email. No popups. No "schedule a 15-minute discovery call" floating buttons. If you want to talk, the letter to the reader on the inner sleeve has every way to reach us.

"High-fidelity is a feeling, not just a resolution."

— Studio principle, [draft]

Four sides to the studio

The Art of the Rescue. We step into systems that are already on fire — slow queries, cascading outages, ten-year-old PHP — and make them stable. We don't replace what we don't have to. We diagnose root causes, not symptoms.

From concept to shipped. Greenfield features, integrations, MVPs, full applications. Code that's documented, tested, and handed back to you clean. The kind your next engineer will not curse us for.

Make the tedious disappear. Scripts, pipelines, AI workflows, internal tools. If a human is currently doing it on a spreadsheet at [02:00 AM] on the [last day of the month], we can probably make that not happen.

Deploy-ready codebases. Pre-built, documented, tested repositories you own outright. Browse what's available or commission a custom build.

You'll know whether we're a fit by the end of the first call. If we're not, we'll tell you so, and recommend someone who is.

Who we are

Toledo Technologies was founded in [Year] by [Name], after a decade of watching good engineers get pulled into bad processes. The pitch is straightforward: hire one studio, get one studio. No bench engineers we've never met getting routed onto your project. No subcontracting chain. The person you scope with is the person who pushes the commit.

We're remote. We've been remote since the studio opened. That isn't a perk we offer — it's a constraint we've built around. It means lower overhead, longer focus blocks, and the discipline to deliver without anyone leaning over our shoulder.

How we work

[01]

Scope

One call. We listen, take notes, ask the questions that get the budget right. You leave with a written estimate, not a "follow-up email."

[02]

Diagnose

We read the code before we write any. For rescues this is half the work. We name the actual problem, in writing, before we touch a thing.

[03]

Build

We work in short, visible cycles. Pull requests are small. Commits are honest. You get a [weekly] note that fits on one screen.

[04]

Hand off

Documentation, tests, runbooks. The next engineer — yours, ours, anyone — should be able to read the repo and understand it cold.

What we will not do

We will not pad invoices with junior labor. We will not run a "discovery phase" priced like the build. We will not accept a project we can't ship. We will not stay quiet when something is going wrong; you will hear about it the same day we do.

We will also not pretend to do things we don't do. We don't run paid ads. We don't write your marketing copy. We don't run your data team. If your project needs that, we'll point you to people who do it well and step out of the way.

The simplest description: we are the studio you call when you have stopped trusting the last [three] you called.

A4Case Study[X:XX]

The art of the
rescue: how we
stabilized a system
falling in real time.

A [series-B] startup. A core service melting under [3×] expected load. A team of [four] engineers who hadn't slept in [a week]. We came in on a Tuesday.

FIG·01 — system architecture (before) [ HIGH-RES ARCHITECTURE DIAGRAM — 21:9 ]

By the time the first email reached us, the user-facing product had been intermittently down for [eleven] days. Not the dramatic kind of down — the slow kind. Pages took [sixteen] seconds to load. Half of API calls timed out. The status page said "all systems operational" because the status page was wired to a healthcheck nobody had updated since [the 2022 migration].

The CTO had run out of ideas, and more importantly, had run out of sleep. Their last [three] consultants had each filed a long Notion document and disappeared. We were the [fourth] phone call. The brief, in full: "Make it stop happening. We can't keep doing this."

Diagnosis before treatment

The instinct, when called into a fire, is to start spraying water. We do the opposite. The first day on a rescue is reading. We pull the codebase, we sit with it, we read. We trace the request path. We diff the deploys. We look at the database before we look at the application, because in [eight out of ten] cases the database is the answer.

In this case, it was. A migration [eighteen months earlier] had introduced a join across two tables that had since each grown by [40×]. The query had run in [80 ms] then; it ran in [12 seconds] now. Every page that called it stalled out at the same point. The application had been working around it with retries — which doubled the load, which made the next retries longer, which doubled the load again.

"They didn't have a performance problem. They had a join that had aged badly, and an architecture that punished the symptom instead of the cause."

— [Engineer], internal note, day [03]

What we changed, in order

  1. Replaced the offending join with a denormalized read-side cache, refreshed on write. Page load dropped from [16s] to [340ms] within [six hours].
  2. Removed the silent retry layer. Failures became loud, which made them fixable.
  3. Wired the status page to actual user-facing latency, not the [/health] endpoint.
  4. Wrote a runbook so the team could do all of the above next time, without us.

By the end of the second week, [98.6%] of requests resolved under [500ms]. The CTO went home. The [3 AM] pages stopped.

What we didn't do

We did not rewrite the service. We did not introduce a new framework. We did not propose a [six-month] migration to a different language. The temptation, on a rescue, is always to use the moment to pitch a bigger engagement. We don't. The job is to make it stop. Bigger conversations come later, if at all.

You read the README first. Every codebase ships with a written tour — what it is, what it isn't, what you'd use it for, and a frank section on what it doesn't do. We'd rather lose a sale than ship a repository to someone who needed something else.

Pricing is one number. No tiers. No "starter / pro / enterprise." You pay it; you get the source. We do not run a SaaS underneath. We do not collect telemetry from your deployment. We do not have a button labeled "upgrade" hidden in the admin UI.
